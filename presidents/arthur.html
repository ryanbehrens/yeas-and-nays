21st president of the United States · 1881–1885

Chester A. Arthur

RepublicanVice president: NoneNot nominated for a full term in 1884

A product of Senator Roscoe Conkling's New York machine whom Hayes had fired as customs collector. He became president when Garfield died and surprised his critics by signing the Pendleton Act, which created the merit-based civil service. He also signed the Chinese Exclusion Act, began rebuilding the Navy, and hid that he had a fatal kidney disease.

Controversies and scandals

What happened, what was proven, and how it ended.

The Chinese Exclusion ActDisputed1882

After vetoing a 20-year version, Arthur signed a 10-year ban on Chinese laborers, the first U.S. law to bar immigrants by nationality.

Outcome: It was extended and made permanent, and repealed in 1943. Congress formally expressed regret in 2011 and 2012.

Source
The Star Route trialsCharged1882–1883

Contractors and postal officials were accused of padding payments for rural mail routes by millions of dollars.

Outcome: All the main defendants were acquitted.

Source
The New York Custom House machineProven1871–1878

As collector of the Port of New York, Arthur ran a patronage operation in which employees were hired for party loyalty and expected to pay political assessments.

Outcome: A federal commission found the office overstaffed and politicized in 1877; Hayes fired him in 1878.

Source
Hiding a fatal illnessProven1882–1885

Arthur learned he had Bright's disease, a fatal kidney condition, early in his term and kept it secret.

Outcome: He made only a token effort for the 1884 nomination and died in November 1886.

Source

How this page is made. Every president gets the same sections and the same checklist. A controversy is listed if it led to an investigation by Congress, an inspector general, a special or independent counsel or a court, or was a sustained national story covered across the political spectrum. Labels follow the strongest official finding, not media coverage.
