George Washington

No party (Federalist-leaning)Vice president: John AdamsDeclined a third term

Party colors follow the site's ideology adjustment: before 1897, Democrats were generally the more conservative party, so they are shown in red and Republicans in blue.

Commander of the Continental Army and the only president elected unanimously, twice. He created the cabinet and set the two-term tradition. He backed Alexander Hamilton's plan to take on the states' war debts, create a national bank and tax whiskey, led troops to put down the Whiskey Rebellion, kept the country neutral in Europe's wars and signed the unpopular Jay Treaty with Britain.

SlaveholdingProven1743–1799

At his death, 317 enslaved people lived at Mount Vernon, 123 of them owned by Washington. As president in Philadelphia he rotated enslaved workers out of Pennsylvania to avoid a state law that would have freed them, and pursued Ona Judge after she escaped in 1796. He signed the Fugitive Slave Act of 1793.

Outcome: His will freed the people he owned after his wife's death; Martha Washington freed them in 1801. He was the only slaveholding founding president to do so.

Source
Putting down the Whiskey RebellionDisputed1794

Washington led about 13,000 militia into western Pennsylvania to end armed resistance to the whiskey tax, the only time a sitting president has led troops in the field.

Outcome: The rebellion collapsed without a battle; the two men convicted of treason were pardoned.

Source
The Jay TreatyDisputed1795

The treaty avoided war with Britain but gave up on many American demands; it was so unpopular that Chief Justice John Jay said he could travel at night by the light of his burning effigies.

Outcome: Ratified 20–10; the fight helped create the first political parties.

Source

How this page is made. Every president gets the same sections and the same checklist. A controversy is listed if it led to an investigation by Congress, an inspector general, a special or independent counsel or a court, or was a sustained national story covered across the political spectrum. Labels follow the strongest official finding, not media coverage. Debt figures come from the U.S. Treasury; deficits and GDP from the White House budget office and the Commerce Department; unemployment from the Bureau of Labor Statistics (before 1948, yearly estimates by Stanley Lebergott via NBER and the Census Bureau's Historical Statistics); prices from MeasuringWorth; executive orders from the Federal Register and National Archives; clemency from the Justice Department.
