31st president of the United States · 1929–1933

Herbert Hoover

RepublicanVice president: Charles CurtisLost re-election in 1932

A mining engineer famous for organizing food relief in World War I. The stock market crashed seven months into his term. He signed the Smoot–Hawley tariff, created the Reconstruction Finance Corporation to lend to banks and businesses, and raised taxes sharply in 1932. Unemployment rose from about 3% to nearly 25%, and he lost to Franklin Roosevelt in a landslide.

Pardons and commutations

A pardon wipes out the punishment for a federal crime; a commutation shortens a sentence but leaves the conviction. Counts are by federal fiscal year (July to June). The Justice Department counts fiscal 1929, including Hoover's first four months, under Coolidge, and all of fiscal 1933, including Roosevelt's first four months, under Hoover.

Controversies and scandals

Routing the Bonus ArmyProvenJuly 1932

Thousands of World War I veterans camped in Washington to demand early payment of a promised bonus. After police killed two veterans, Hoover ordered the Army to clear the area; troops under MacArthur used tanks, cavalry and tear gas and burned the camp.

Outcome: No one was charged; the scene hurt Hoover badly in the 1932 election.

Source
The Smoot–Hawley tariffDisputedJune 1930

More than 1,000 economists urged him to veto the tariff increases; he signed them, and trading partners retaliated.

Outcome: World trade fell sharply; how much the tariff deepened the Depression is debated.

Source
Mexican repatriationProven1929–1933

Labor Department raids and local pressure drove hundreds of thousands of Mexicans and Mexican Americans, many of them U.S. citizens, to leave the country. The effort continued after Hoover left office.

Outcome: California formally apologized in 2005.

Source
Raising taxes in a depressionDisputedJune 1932

The Revenue Act of 1932 raised the top income tax rate from 25% to 63% to close the deficit.

Outcome: Supporters called it responsible; many economists say it made the downturn worse.

Source

How this page is made. Every president gets the same sections and the same checklist. A controversy is listed if it led to an investigation by Congress, an inspector general, a special or independent counsel or a court, or was a sustained national story covered across the political spectrum. Labels follow the strongest official finding, not media coverage. Debt figures come from the U.S. Treasury; deficits and GDP from the White House budget office and the Commerce Department; unemployment from the Bureau of Labor Statistics (before 1948, the Census Bureau's Historical Statistics); prices from MeasuringWorth; executive orders from the Federal Register and National Archives; clemency from the Justice Department.
