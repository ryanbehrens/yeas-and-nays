The Lines

Every ten years, and lately more often, someone redraws the map of House districts. Where the lines go can decide who wins before anyone votes. Here's how it works, how it got this way, and every state's maps since 1789.

Same voters, different maps

Fifty voters: 30 blue, 20 red. They need five districts of ten. Tap a map to redraw the lines.

Packing crowds the other side's voters into a few districts they win by huge margins. Cracking spreads the rest thin so they fall short everywhere else. Do both and a minority of the votes can win a majority of the seats.

A short history of the gerrymander

  1. 1812
    The word is born

    Massachusetts Governor Elbridge Gerry signs a state senate map that twists one district around Boston's northern suburbs. A Boston Gazette cartoon draws it as a salamander, the "Gerry-mander." The tactic was older than the name.

  2. 1842
    One district, one member

    Congress requires states to elect House members from single-member districts of contiguous territory instead of statewide, so the lines start to matter everywhere.

  3. 1872–1911
    Equal and compact

    Later apportionment laws add rules: districts should hold as nearly equal populations as practicable (1872) and be compact (1901, renewed in 1911).

  4. 1929
    The rules lapse

    The Reapportionment Act fixes the House at 435 seats but drops the size and shape rules; the Supreme Court confirms they expired (Wood v. Broom, 1932). Many states stop redrawing at all, and rural districts end up with far fewer people than city ones.

  5. 1962–64
    One person, one vote

    Baker v. Carr lets federal courts hear districting cases. Wesberry v. Sanders (1964) requires congressional districts of nearly equal population, and Reynolds v. Sims does the same for state legislatures. States must redraw after every census. On The Bench →

  6. 1965 & 1982
    The Voting Rights Act

    The Act bans voting rules that discriminate by race; the 1982 amendments let maps be challenged for diluting minority votes even without proof of intent. Thornburg v. Gingles (1986) sets the test, leading to majority-minority districts.

  7. 1986
    Partisan gerrymanders: maybe

    Davis v. Bandemer says partisan gerrymandering claims can be heard in court, but no majority agrees on a test. No map is ever struck down under it.

  8. 1993
    Race can't be the main reason

    Shaw v. Reno: North Carolina's snaking 12th District, drawn to give Black voters a second majority district, can be challenged as a racial gerrymander. Some of the least compact districts ever drawn date from this decade.

  9. 2004–06
    No test, and mid-decade redraws allowed

    Vieth v. Jubelirer (2004) finds no workable standard for partisan gerrymanders. LULAC v. Perry (2006) lets Texas redraw in the middle of a decade, though one district violated the Voting Rights Act.

  10. 2010–12
    The data era

    Detailed voter data and mapping software let map-drawers predict outcomes precisely. After the 2010 census, Republicans, having won many state legislatures, draw most maps; Democrats do the same where they control them.

  11. 2013
    Preclearance ends

    Shelby County v. Holder frees states with a history of discrimination from needing federal approval before changing maps. On The Bench →

  12. 2015–18
    Commissions and state courts

    Arizona State Legislature v. AIRC (2015) lets voters hand map-drawing to independent commissions. In 2018 Pennsylvania's Supreme Court throws out the state's map under the state constitution, and Michigan and Colorado voters create commissions.

  13. 2019
    Federal courts step out

    Rucho v. Common Cause (5–4): partisan gerrymandering is a political question federal courts can't decide. Challenges move to state courts and state constitutions.

  14. 2023–24
    The Voting Rights Act, again

    Allen v. Milligan (2023) orders Alabama to draw a second district where Black voters can elect their choice. Moore v. Harper (2023) keeps state courts able to review maps. Alexander v. South Carolina NAACP (2024) gives legislatures a presumption of good faith.

  15. 2025–26
    The mid-decade map war

    Texas redraws in mid-decade to add Republican seats; California voters answer with Proposition 50. After Louisiana v. Callais (April 2026, 6–3) narrows race-conscious districts under the Voting Rights Act, more states redraw. Alabama, California, Florida, Louisiana, North Carolina, Ohio, Tennessee, Texas and Utah vote under new maps in 2026.

What the numbers can't tell you

Odd shapes aren't proof. Coastlines, rivers, county lines and the Voting Rights Act's requirements all bend districts. A compact map can still be gerrymandered; a sprawling one can be fair.

Votes vs. seats isn't proof either. Democratic voters cluster in cities, so even neutral lines tend to give them fewer seats than their vote share. A few points of gap is normal under any single-member system; the winning party usually gets a seat bonus.

Uncontested races skew vote totals. When one party doesn't run, that district's votes all go one way, and some states don't count votes in uncontested races at all. Those districts are left out of the efficiency gap here.

What it does show: when a state's seats stay lopsided across elections in which the votes swing back and forth, or when its maps change sharply right after one party takes control of redistricting, the lines are doing work.

The 120th Congress uses the 2026 results; the nine states with new 2026 maps aren't in the boundary data yet.

Compactness is the Polsby-Popper score, 4π × area ÷ perimeter², measured on each district's shape on the globe. Boundaries are simplified to about 1 km, which smooths coastlines; scores compare well with each other but run a little higher than ones from full-detail maps.

Votes: MIT Election Data and Science Lab, U.S. House 1976–2024 (Harvard Dataverse, CC0). Vote share is the Democratic share of Democratic plus Republican votes. The efficiency gap counts each party's "wasted" votes (every vote for a loser, and a winner's votes beyond the half needed) and divides the difference by all votes cast; shown only for states with at least two districts, leaving out uncontested races.
